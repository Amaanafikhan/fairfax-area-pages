## Long Branch, Fairfax County, Virginia: Geography, History, Housing Stock and Assessment Framework

*A reference study of the unincorporated census-designated place between Little River Turnpike and Braddock Road, organized around the stream valley that gives the community its name.*

## Executive Summary

Long Branch is a census-designated place (CDP) in central Fairfax County, Virginia, occupying roughly two and a half square miles of rolling Piedmont terrain directly southeast of the independent City of Fairfax. It is bounded by Little River Turnpike (Virginia Route 236) on the north, Guinea Road on the east, Braddock Road on the south, Burke Station Road on the west, and the corporate limits of the City of Fairfax on the northwest.

Despite the Fairfax mailing address carried by most of its homes, Long Branch is not part of the City of Fairfax. It is unincorporated territory governed directly by the Fairfax County Board of Supervisors, served by county police, fire and rescue, and Fairfax County Public Schools, and assessed by the county's Department of Tax Administration. The community takes its name from Long Branch, a small tributary of Accotink Creek whose valley shapes the street pattern, the parkland and the drainage characteristics of nearly every lot.

### Key Takeaways

- Governance: unincorporated Fairfax County within the Braddock Magisterial District; not part of the independent City of Fairfax.
- Boundaries: Little River Turnpike (VA-236) north, Guinea Road east, Braddock Road south, Burke Station Road west, City of Fairfax limits northwest.
- Watershed: the Long Branch stream, a tributary of Accotink Creek, organizes the community's topography and stream valley parkland.
- Housing: predominantly 1960s and 1970s detached houses — colonials, split-levels, split-foyers and ramblers — with scattered later infill.
- Schools: W.T. Woodson High School, Robert Frost Middle School, Olde Creek Elementary and Little Run Elementary are physically located within the CDP; attendance must be confirmed with the FCPS Boundary Locator.
- Commuter gateways: Little River Turnpike and Braddock Road toward the Capital Beltway, Fairfax City routes toward I-66, and bus links to the Vienna/Fairfax-GMU Metrorail station.

### Why Addresses Read “Fairfax” or “Annandale”

Mailing addresses in Virginia are assigned by the United States Postal Service according to the post office that delivers the mail, not according to county, city or CDP lines. Most Long Branch homes fall within the Fairfax 22032 delivery area, so they carry a “Fairfax, VA” mailing address even though they lie outside the City of Fairfax. Near the eastern edge, delivery areas associated with Annandale (22003) can apply to some streets. A postal place name therefore says nothing about which government taxes a property, which police department responds, or which schools serve it.

### Distinction from the City of Fairfax

Virginia is one of the few states in which cities are independent of the counties around them. The City of Fairfax is a separate political subdivision with its own council, tax rate, assessor, police department and school board arrangement, even though it is geographically enclosed by Fairfax County and serves as the county seat. Properties in Long Branch are assessed and taxed by Fairfax County, are subject to the county Zoning Ordinance and Comprehensive Plan, and appear in county — not city — land records. Buyers comparing listings near the northwest edge should verify jurisdiction on the county's parcel records rather than relying on a mailing address.

## Hydrology and Topography

The community's name is hydrological. Long Branch is a small perennial stream that gathers runoff from the residential land between Little River Turnpike and Braddock Road and carries it southeast toward Accotink Creek, one of the principal drainages of central Fairfax County. Accotink Creek in turn flows through Lake Accotink and Fort Belvoir before reaching the Potomac River estuary, which places every lot in Long Branch within the Chesapeake Bay watershed.

### Piedmont Terrain

Long Branch sits in the Piedmont physiographic province, the belt of rolling uplands underlain by weathered crystalline rock that extends west from the Fall Line. The terrain is characterized by rounded ridges separated by incised stream valleys. Subdivision streets commonly run along ridgelines and contours, with cul-de-sacs terminating above the wooded stream corridors. As a result, many lots slope noticeably front to back or side to side, a condition that explains the prevalence of split-level and walk-out basement designs in the housing stock.

### Stream Valley Buffers and Resource Protection Areas

Fairfax County implements the Chesapeake Bay Preservation Act through its Chesapeake Bay Preservation Ordinance (Chapter 118 of the County Code). The ordinance designates Resource Protection Areas (RPAs) along perennial streams and connected wetlands, generally including a vegetated buffer of at least 100 feet measured from the stream edge. Within an RPA, new impervious surfaces, additions, accessory structures and clearing of vegetation are restricted, and many changes require an exception or a water quality impact assessment. Because the Long Branch corridor and its tributaries thread through the community, a meaningful share of rear yards abut or intersect RPA land.

- RPA status is mapped parcel by parcel on the county's GIS and should be checked before planning decks, additions, pools or sheds.
- Lots adjoining stream valley parkland may also border Fairfax County Park Authority land, where private encroachment is prohibited.
- Floodplain designations, where they apply, impose separate construction limits and may affect flood insurance requirements.

## Historical Evolution

### The Ravensworth Grant

In 1685 William Fitzhugh of Stafford County acquired a tract of nearly 22,000 acres in the then-unsettled interior of northern Virginia. Known as Ravensworth, it was among the largest single colonial holdings in what would become Fairfax County, and its lands extended across much of the central county, including the ground on which Long Branch now stands. Ravensworth was subdivided among Fitzhugh heirs over the following two centuries, and its successive divisions shaped the property lines later inherited by farms and, eventually, suburban developers.

### Little River Turnpike as the Historic Spine

The northern edge of the community follows one of Virginia's earliest improved roads. The Little River Turnpike Company was chartered by the General Assembly in 1802 to build a toll road westward from Alexandria toward the Little River near Aldie, giving farmers in the Piedmont a dependable route to the port. The turnpike, now Route 236, passed through the courthouse settlement that became the City of Fairfax and remains the principal arterial along Long Branch's northern frontage.

### Agricultural Era

Through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the land between the turnpike and Braddock Road was farmland and woodland. As Washington grew, Fairfax County became a significant dairy-producing county, and farms in the central county supplied milk to the capital region. Fields occupied the ridges while the steeper stream valleys remained wooded — a pattern that survives today in the forested Long Branch corridor that subdivision designers chose to leave undeveloped.

### Suburbanization, 1960s–1970s

Large-scale residential construction arrived in the 1960s and continued through the 1970s, driven by federal employment growth, the opening of the Capital Beltway in Virginia in 1964, and the expansion of public schools to serve young families. Farms were sold and platted into subdivisions of detached houses on quarter-acre to half-acre lots. W.T. Woodson High School, named for longtime Fairfax County schools superintendent Wilbert Tucker Woodson, opened in this period to serve the new neighborhoods.

### Shifting Census Geography

The name Long Branch has been used by the Census Bureau for more than one configuration of territory. Census geography in this part of the county has been redrawn repeatedly as new CDPs were created and existing ones subdivided, and comparisons of Long Branch figures across decades are only meaningful when the boundary in use for each census is taken into account. The present, compact CDP described on this page reflects the 2010 delineation.

### Proximity to George Mason University

George Mason University began as a branch of the University of Virginia in the 1950s, moved to its Fairfax campus in the 1960s, and became an independent university in 1972. Its growth into a major public research university transformed the western edge of the area: Braddock Road traffic, the university's employment base, and demand for housing within commuting distance of campus are all part of the community's modern context.

### How the Pyramid System Works

FCPS organizes schools into pyramids: each high school is fed by one or more middle schools, which are in turn fed by a set of elementary schools. A pyramid is named for its high school. Elementary attendance areas do not always nest neatly inside a single middle or high school area; some elementary schools are split feeders whose students continue to different middle or high schools depending on address. The location of a school within the CDP therefore does not guarantee that a particular address is assigned to it.

### Advanced Academic Programs

FCPS provides Advanced Academic Programs (AAP) through a continuum of services, including full-time Level IV centers that may be housed at a school other than a student's base school. Eligibility is determined through the FCPS screening process, and AAP center assignments follow separate boundaries from general attendance areas.

## Real Estate Tax Code and Assessment Cycles

Because Long Branch lies in unincorporated Fairfax County, its properties are valued and taxed entirely under the county's system; the City of Fairfax assessor has no role. The framework rests on state law and a predictable annual calendar rather than on market statistics.

### The Fair Market Value Mandate

Section 58.1-3201 of the Code of Virginia requires that all real estate be assessed at 100 percent of fair market value, and it obliges localities to assess uniformly. Fairfax County satisfies this through annual reassessment: the Department of Tax Administration (DTA) estimates the value of each parcel as of January 1 using mass-appraisal models built from sales of comparable properties and physical characteristics recorded in county files.

### The Annual Cycle

- January 1: valuation date for the tax year.
- Late winter: DTA mails assessment notices and publishes values in its online real estate database.
- Spring: the Board of Supervisors adopts the real estate tax rate with the county budget.
- July 28: first-half real estate tax installment due.
- December 5: second-half real estate tax installment due.

### Reviewing and Appealing an Assessment

An owner who believes an assessment exceeds fair market value or is not uniform with similar properties may first request an administrative review by DTA, supported by evidence such as comparable sales or documentation of physical defects. If the matter is not resolved, the owner may appeal to the Fairfax County Board of Equalization (BOE), an independent citizen board established under Article 14 of Chapter 32 of Title 58.1 of the Code of Virginia. Further appeal lies to the Circuit Court under Section 58.1-3984. Filing deadlines are published annually by DTA and the BOE and must be met for each tax year.

Fairfax County also administers tax relief programs for qualifying older and disabled homeowners and for disabled veterans as authorized by state law; eligibility rules are published by DTA.

## Ravensworth, Fairfax County, Virginia: The Fitzhugh Grant, the Lost Mansion and an Early-1960s Suburb

*A reference study of the compact census-designated place inside the Beltway's southwest quadrant, from a seventeenth-century land grant to a mid-century subdivision on the shores of Lake Accotink.*

## Executive Summary

Ravensworth is a compact census-designated place of just under one square mile in east-central Fairfax County, Virginia, set inside the southwest quadrant formed by the Capital Beltway and Braddock Road. It is framed by Braddock Road on the north, the Beltway on the northeast, Accotink Creek on the west, Lake Accotink on the south and Flag Run on the southeast. Homes carry Springfield, Virginia 22151 mailing addresses, although the CDP is a separate statistical area from the Springfield CDP.

The community bears the name of one of the most consequential properties in northern Virginia history: Ravensworth, the vast tract acquired by William Fitzhugh in 1685, and the plantation house built on it around 1796 that sheltered members of the Lee family before burning in 1926. The modern neighborhood is largely the product of the early-1960s Ravensworth Farm subdivision, a coherent collection of brick ramblers, split-levels and colonials on wooded lots that slope toward the lake.

## Historical Evolution

Few Fairfax County communities can trace their name so directly to a single historic estate. The history of Ravensworth spans more than three centuries and connects a colonial land speculator, a federal-era plantation house, the family of Robert E. Lee and the mid-twentieth-century transformation of Fairfax County from farmland to suburb.

### The 1685 Fitzhugh Grant

In 1685 William Fitzhugh, a lawyer and planter of Stafford County, acquired nearly 22,000 acres in the interior of what was then Stafford County and later became Fairfax County. The tract, which he named Ravensworth, is frequently described as the largest single colonial holding in the future county. Fitzhugh never lived on the property. His surviving correspondence records an intention to settle French Protestant refugees — Huguenots — on the land as tenants, part of a broader effort by Virginia proprietors to populate frontier holdings that otherwise produced no income.

### Division Among Fitzhugh Heirs

Fitzhugh's will divided Ravensworth among his descendants, and over the eighteenth century the tract was split into large family seats. Three manor houses eventually stood on Ravensworth land: Ossian Hall, built around 1783 by Nicholas Fitzhugh; Oak Hill, built around 1790 by Richard Fitzhugh; and the Ravensworth mansion itself. Ossian Hall was demolished in 1959 for residential development, and Oak Hill survives as the only one of the three still standing.

### The Ravensworth Mansion, c. 1796

Around 1796 William Fitzhugh of Chatham — a descendant of the original grantee and a member of the Continental Congress — built a frame Palladian-style mansion on the Ravensworth estate, to which he retired from his Stafford County seat. The house became the center of an estate of roughly 12,000 acres, a fraction of the original grant but still among the largest working properties in the county.

### The Lee Family Connection

Through intermarriage the Fitzhughs were closely tied to the Custis and Lee families; Mary Anna Randolph Custis, who married Robert E. Lee, was a granddaughter of William Fitzhugh of Chatham. Ravensworth served the extended family as a refuge on several occasions. Anne Hill Carter Lee, Robert E. Lee's mother, died at Ravensworth in 1829. In the spring of 1861, as Union forces prepared to occupy Arlington, Mary Custis Lee left her home there and stayed at Ravensworth before moving further south. After the death of Anna Maria Fitzhugh, the widow of William Henry Fitzhugh, in 1874, the estate passed to Lee heirs, and descendants of Robert E. Lee held the property into the twentieth century.

### Destruction by Fire, August 1, 1926

On August 1, 1926, the Ravensworth mansion was destroyed by a fire whose cause was never determined; some contemporaries regarded it as suspicious. A number of family portraits had already been removed or were carried out during the blaze, but many others were lost. Dr. George Bolling Lee later built a smaller farmhouse on the property. The site of the mansion now lies within the light-industrial area along Port Royal Road, a short distance southeast of the Ravensworth Shopping Center.

### From Estate to Subdivision

The remaining Ravensworth acreage passed out of family hands in the decades after World War II, as the Capital Beltway, federal employment and a surge of young families transformed the Braddock District. In the early 1960s the Ravensworth Farm subdivision was developed on part of the old estate between Braddock Road and Lake Accotink. Its name, its curving streets and its brick houses define the community today.

### Recording the Lost House

Because the house burned before the Historic American Buildings Survey (HABS) was established in 1933, knowledge of its appearance rests chiefly on earlier photographs, family papers and descriptions now held in archival collections, including those of the Library of Congress, whose HABS and Prints and Photographs holdings are the starting point for research on surviving Fitzhugh-era buildings in the county. Local historical scholarship, notably the Braddock District heritage archives and the project published as The Story of Ravensworth, has compiled the deeds, family records and site histories of the entire grant.

## Real Estate Tax Code and Assessment Cycles

Ravensworth homeowners are taxed by Fairfax County alone; there is no town or special municipal layer. The relevant legal framework is set out in the Code of Virginia and administered by the county Department of Tax Administration (DTA).

### Assessment at Full Fair Market Value

Virginia Code Section 58.1-3201 directs that real estate be assessed at 100 percent of fair market value. In a homogeneous subdivision such as Ravensworth Farm, DTA's mass-appraisal models draw heavily on sales of similar houses in the same and nearby neighborhoods, adjusted for differences in living area, lot characteristics, condition and improvements on file. Unpermitted improvements, or permitted work not yet reflected in county records, can cause recorded characteristics to diverge from reality.

### Calendar

- Valuation date: January 1 of each tax year.
- Assessment notices: mailed by DTA in late winter, with values posted online.
- Tax rate: adopted each spring by the Board of Supervisors.
- Installments: due July 28 and December 5.

### Appeals

The first step in contesting an assessment is an administrative appeal to DTA, which can correct factual errors in the property record as well as consider valuation evidence. The next level is the Fairfax County Board of Equalization, a panel of appointed citizens that hears appeals under the Code of Virginia's provisions on boards of equalization. Owners may also seek review in the Fairfax County Circuit Court under Section 58.1-3984. Each level has its own annual filing deadline, published by DTA and the BOE.

## West Springfield, Fairfax County, Virginia: Between Accotink and Pohick Creeks

## Executive Summary

West Springfield is a census-designated place of roughly 4.8 square miles in southern Fairfax County, Virginia, lying west of the Springfield commercial core and organized around two creeks. Accotink Creek defines its eastern edge and Pohick Creek, together with a power-line corridor, much of its western edge. The Norfolk Southern rail line used by the Virginia Railway Express Manassas Line forms the northern boundary, and Fort Belvoir's North Area and the Fairfax County Parkway form the southern boundary.

The CDP is unincorporated Fairfax County; it has no town government, and homes carry Springfield mailing addresses, principally in ZIP code 22152. Its identity is defined less by commerce than by the residential subdivisions built between the 1960s and the 1980s along Old Keene Mill Road and Rolling Road, and by a dense cluster of public schools, including West Springfield High School.

- Distinct from Springfield: West Springfield is its own CDP west of the Springfield CDP; the two share the Springfield postal name.
- Boundaries: rail line (VRE Manassas Line) north, Accotink Creek east, Fort Belvoir and the Fairfax County Parkway south, Pohick Creek and a power-line corridor west.
- Governance: unincorporated Fairfax County; county services, zoning and taxation apply.
- Housing: subdivisions of the 1960s through 1980s — ramblers, split-levels, colonials, contemporaries and townhouse sections.

## Historical Evolution

### Colonial Land Patterns

Southern Fairfax County was patented in large colonial tracts during the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The great Ravensworth grant of William Fitzhugh, acquired in 1685, covered much of the central county to the north and east, and the lands of the broader Springfield–Burke region descend from that and neighboring patents through successive divisions among heirs and later sales. For two centuries the area between Accotink and Pohick creeks remained a landscape of farms, woodlots and mills connected by rural roads.

### Old Keene Mill Road

Old Keene Mill Road, the community's principal east–west street, preserves the name of a historic mill and follows an alignment that predates suburban development. Rural roads such as this one, together with Rolling Road, became the framework onto which twentieth-century subdivisions were attached, which is why many neighborhoods are organized as clusters of curving streets branching off a few older through routes.

### Suburban Boom, 1960s–1980s

Residential development spread west from the Springfield core across Accotink Creek beginning in the 1960s and accelerated through the 1970s, a period of rapid growth across southern Fairfax County. New elementary schools opened in quick succession, and West Springfield High School was established to serve the expanding population. Construction continued into the 1980s in the western and southern sections, where later subdivisions introduced contemporary designs and townhouse communities.

### Fairfax County Parkway

The Fairfax County Parkway (Virginia Route 286) was planned as a north–south connector linking Route 7 near Reston to Route 1 near Fort Belvoir and was built in segments from the late 1980s onward. Its completion through the former Engineer Proving Ground gave West Springfield a direct link to the western county and to the Springfield Interchange area without relying on local streets.

## Real Estate Tax Code and Assessment Cycles

Property in West Springfield is assessed and taxed solely by Fairfax County. Owners of townhouses in planned sections pay association assessments separately; those are private obligations under recorded documents and are unrelated to county real estate taxes.

### Legal Basis

Under Virginia Code Section 58.1-3201, real estate must be assessed at 100 percent of fair market value. The Fairfax County Department of Tax Administration (DTA) carries this out annually, valuing each parcel as of January 1. In communities like West Springfield that contain both detached and attached housing built across several decades, DTA's models distinguish among property types and neighborhoods, so values for a townhouse section and an adjacent detached subdivision can move differently from year to year.

### Annual Calendar

- January 1 — effective date of valuation.
- Late winter — DTA issues assessment notices and publishes values online.
- Spring — Board of Supervisors sets the tax rate during budget adoption.
- July 28 — first-half tax installment due.
- December 5 — second-half tax installment due.

### Appeal Path

Owners may request an administrative review from DTA, presenting comparable sales, appraisals or evidence of errors in recorded characteristics. If dissatisfied, they may appeal to the Fairfax County Board of Equalization, an independent citizen board created under the Code of Virginia, and thereafter to the Circuit Court under Section 58.1-3984. Annual deadlines for each stage are published by DTA and the BOE.

## Historical Evolution

### Before the Suburb

Developer Edward R. Carr, whose firm built much of the greater Springfield area after World War II, obtained the permits for North Springfield, and construction began in 1954. The Virginia section of the Capital Beltway opened in 1964 and reshaped North Springfield's western and southern edges. ## Executive Summary

Burke Centre is a planned residential community and census-designated place in southwestern Fairfax County, Virginia, within ZIP code 22015. ## Historical Evolution

### Ravensworth Origins

The colonial land history of the Burke area is bound up with the Ravensworth grant acquired by William Fitzhugh in 1685, a tract of nearly 22,000 acres extending across central Fairfax County. The grant was divided among Fitzhugh heirs and successive owners over the following centuries, and the farms that eventually stood on the Burke Centre site descend from those divisions and from neighboring patents.

### Silas Burke and Burke's Station

The name Burke comes from Silas Burke, a nineteenth-century landowner, farmer and local official. When the Orange and Alexandria Railroad was built through the area in the early 1850s, a stop on his land became known as Burke's Station. During the Civil War the station was the target of a December 1862 raid by Confederate cavalry under J.E.B. Stuart, remembered for the telegram Stuart sent from the station's telegraph office to the Union quartermaster general complaining about the quality of captured mules. The same rail corridor is used today by VRE and Amtrak.

### The Airport That Was Not Built

In 1951 federal aviation officials announced plans to build Washington's second major airport at Burke, and in June of that year notice was posted at the Burke post office that the government would begin condemning roughly 4,500 acres. Residents organized against the plan, forming the Burke Civic Association, and the fight continued through much of the decade. In 1958 President Dwight D. Eisenhower approved a site near Chantilly instead, and the new airport — Washington Dulles International — opened there in 1962.

### From Condemned Land to Park and Planned Community

Having acquired land at Burke, the federal government had to dispose of it. A substantial portion went to the Fairfax County Park Authority and became Burke Lake Park; other tracts were sold, including land acquired by private developers. That released acreage made possible a large, comprehensively planned community rather than piecemeal subdivision, and in the 1970s the Burke Centre plan took shape, with its own governing association, open space network and neighborhood structure.

### Incorporation of the Conservancy, 1976

The Burke Centre Conservancy was incorporated in June 1976 to own and maintain the community's common areas and administer its covenants. Construction proceeded neighborhood by neighborhood over the following decades. In September 2018, a statement entered in the Congressional Record recognized the Conservancy and summarized the community's scale and facilities.

### Zoning

Large portions of Burke Centre were developed under Fairfax County's planned development zoning, principally the Planned Residential Community (PRC) district, which permits a mix of housing types and densities under an overall approved plan; other areas may fall under Planned Development Housing (PDH) or other districts. In planned districts, the approved development plan and associated conditions shape lot layout, density and open space. The zoning of any parcel should be confirmed on the official county zoning map.

### Cluster-Level Covenants

In addition to Conservancy-wide documents, many clusters have their own recorded covenants and rules addressing matters such as parking, fences, trash enclosures and townhouse exteriors. A complete review of a Burke Centre property's obligations therefore includes the Conservancy declaration and bylaws, any cluster documents, and current architectural guidelines.

## Structural Anatomy and Technical Maintenance

**Late-1970s and 1980s construction: issues and maintenance priorities**

Homes built before 1978 require federal lead-based paint disclosure at sale. Most of Burke Centre was built after that date, but the earliest sections should be checked against the year of construction recorded by the county.

## Fairfax County Public Schools

Burke Centre lies within the area served by two of Fairfax County's combined middle and high schools: Robinson Secondary School and Lake Braddock Secondary School. Elementary schools in the vicinity of Burke Centre include Terra Centre Elementary, Fairview Elementary and Bonnie Brae Elementary, among others.

### How Pyramids Apply

In FCPS, elementary schools feed secondary schools in groupings called pyramids. A secondary school such as Robinson or Lake Braddock houses both middle and high school grades, so its pyramid begins at the middle school level within the same building. A planned community does not form a single attendance area: different Burke Centre clusters can be assigned to different elementary and secondary schools, and some elementary schools are split feeders.

> This monograph does not state assignments for any address. Confirm current elementary and secondary assignments, and any Advanced Academic Programs center, with the FCPS Boundary Locator.

## Transportation

### Burke Centre Station

Burke Centre station is one of the original Virginia Railway Express Manassas Line stations that opened with VRE service in June 1992. Commuter trains run toward Alexandria, Crystal City, L'Enfant Plaza and Washington Union Station. A multilevel parking garage with bus bays opened at the station in summer 2008, substantially expanding commuter parking. Amtrak began serving the station with Northeast Regional trains in 2009, and it was listed as a regular Amtrak stop in 2010; the number of Amtrak trains that call there varies by timetable.

## Parks and Environment

Burke Centre's open space is largely private common land owned by the Conservancy rather than county parkland. Its wooded buffers, stream corridors, lake and ponds were set aside as part of the community plan and are maintained through Conservancy assessments.

- Conservancy open space: woodland, meadows and stream buffers between clusters, crossed by the path network.
- Lake and ponds: one lake and six ponds that function as stormwater management facilities as well as amenities; their dams, outfalls and sediment levels require periodic engineering attention.
- Burke Lake Park: a large Fairfax County Park Authority park a short distance south along Burke Lake Road, created in part from land once condemned for the airport.
- Pohick Creek watershed: headwater tributaries of Pohick Creek drain much of the community toward the Potomac River.

Perennial streams within the community are subject to Resource Protection Area rules under Fairfax County's Chesapeake Bay Preservation Ordinance, which restrict construction and clearing within protected buffers. Stormwater ponds that serve as regulated facilities must be maintained to county standards, a long-term obligation that falls on the Conservancy as owner.

## Real Estate Tax Code, Assessment Cycles and Association Disclosure

### County Assessment

Burke Centre properties are assessed by the Fairfax County Department of Tax Administration (DTA) under Virginia Code Section 58.1-3201, which requires assessment at 100 percent of fair market value. DTA values each parcel annually as of January 1, issues notices in late winter, and the Board of Supervisors sets the tax rate each spring. County real estate taxes are payable in two installments, due July 28 and December 5.

### Appeals

Owners who disagree with an assessment may seek administrative review by DTA, then appeal to the Fairfax County Board of Equalization, and thereafter seek judicial review in Circuit Court under Section 58.1-3984. Townhouse owners in particular may find relevant evidence in recent sales of the same model within the same cluster. Each stage has an annual filing deadline published by the county.

### Conservancy Assessments Are Separate

Conservancy assessments — and any separate cluster or condominium charges — are private obligations created by recorded governing documents. They are levied and collected by the associations, not by Fairfax County, and they are not part of the county real estate tax bill. Unpaid association assessments can, however, become a lien on the property under Virginia law.

### Virginia Property Owners' Association Act and Resale Disclosure

The Burke Centre Conservancy is a property owners' association governed by the Virginia Property Owners' Association Act (Code of Virginia, Title 55.1, Chapter 18). When a lot is resold, Virginia's resale disclosure provisions (Title 55.1, Chapter 23) require the seller to obtain a resale certificate — commonly called a disclosure packet — from the association. The packet includes the governing documents, a statement of assessments and any unpaid amounts, and notice of any known violations or pending actions affecting the lot, among other items. The purchaser has a statutory right to review the packet and may cancel the contract within a short period after receiving it as specified by the statute. Units in condominiums are subject to parallel requirements under the Virginia Condominium Act.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are the five neighborhoods of Burke Centre?**

Burke Centre's five neighborhoods are The Woods, The Oaks, The Commons, The Ponds and The Landings. Each is divided into smaller clusters within the Burke Centre Conservancy.

**Is Burke Centre an HOA?**

Yes. Most Burke Centre homes belong to the Burke Centre Conservancy, a mandatory property owners' association incorporated in June 1976 that maintains common areas, facilities and architectural standards under the Virginia Property Owners' Association Act.

**Why wasn't Dulles Airport built in Burke?**

The federal government planned a second Washington airport at Burke in 1951 and began condemning land, but residents organized strong opposition. In 1958 President Eisenhower approved a site near Chantilly instead, where Dulles International Airport opened in 1962.

**When was the Burke Centre Conservancy founded?**

The Burke Centre Conservancy was incorporated in June 1976.

**What is the difference between Burke and Burke Centre?**

Burke Centre is a planned community governed by the Burke Centre Conservancy and its own census-designated place. Burke is the larger surrounding area and a separate CDP. Both are unincorporated Fairfax County and share Burke 22015 mailing addresses.

**Does Burke Centre have a train station?**

Yes. Burke Centre station is an original 1992 VRE Manassas Line stop with a multilevel parking garage opened in 2008, and it has been served by Amtrak Northeast Regional trains since 2009–2010.

**How many miles of paths are in Burke Centre?**

The Burke Centre Conservancy maintains more than 30 miles of paths connecting clusters, ponds, community facilities and the station.

**Do I need approval to change the exterior of a Burke Centre home?**

Generally yes. Exterior changes such as additions, decks, fences, colors, roofing, windows and doors usually require approval through the Conservancy's architectural review process, in addition to any Fairfax County permits.

**Are Burke Centre Conservancy fees part of my property tax?**

No. Conservancy and cluster assessments are private association charges set by the governing documents. Fairfax County real estate taxes are billed separately and are due July 28 and December 5.

**What ZIP code is Burke Centre, VA?**

Burke Centre uses ZIP code 22015 with a Burke, VA mailing address.
